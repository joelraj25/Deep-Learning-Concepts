Unit at a glance

The unit tells one story. A linear model draws one straight boundary. A shallow network stacks such units to draw curved boundaries. Three tools train it: a loss function measures the error, backpropagation finds which weights caused it, and SGD corrects them. The universal approximation theorem says why this can work at all.

Topic 1 of 7 · Linear models

Support Vector Machine (SVM)

1One-line definition

A Support Vector Machine is a supervised linear classifier that finds the hyperplane \(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b=0\) separating two classes with the maximum margin; the training points closest to the hyperplane are called support vectors.

2Diagram to draw

3Interactive demo: drag the points

4Step-by-step explanation

In simple words

  1. Plot the two classes as points.
  2. Many straight lines can separate them. SVM picks the one that leaves the widest empty road between the classes.
  3. The points that touch the edges of the road are the support vectors. Only they decide where the line goes.
  4. A new point is classified by the side of the line it falls on.
  5. If the classes overlap, a few points are allowed inside the road, and each one pays a penalty C (soft margin).

Technical version

  1. The classifier is \(\hat y=\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b)\) with labels \(y_i\in\{-1,+1\}\).
  2. Scale \(\mathbf{w},b\) so the closest points satisfy \(y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i+b)=1\). The margin is then \(2/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\).
  3. Maximising the margin is the same as minimising \(\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2\) subject to \(y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i+b)\ge 1\). This is a convex quadratic program, so it has one global optimum.
  4. Soft margin adds slack variables \(\xi_i\) and the penalty \(C\sum\xi_i\). This equals minimising \(\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2\) plus C times the hinge loss.
  5. Solving the dual gives multipliers \(\alpha_i\) with \(\mathbf{w}=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\mathbf{x}_i\). Only support vectors have \(\alpha_i>0\).
  6. The dual uses the data only as dot products \(\mathbf{x}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}_j\). Replacing them with a kernel \(K(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j)\) gives a non-linear SVM (kernel trick).

5Key formulas

\[\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b=0\qquad\qquad \hat y=\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b)\qquad\qquad \text{margin}=\frac{2}{\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert}\] \[\textbf{Hard margin:}\quad \min_{\mathbf{w},b}\ \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2\quad\text{subject to}\quad y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i+b)\ge 1\] \[\textbf{Soft margin:}\quad \min_{\mathbf{w},b,\xi}\ \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2+C\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_i\quad\text{subject to}\quad y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i+b)\ge 1-\xi_i,\ \ \xi_i\ge 0\] \[\textbf{Hinge loss:}\quad L=\max\bigl(0,\ 1-y\,(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b)\bigr)\]
SymbolMeaning
\(\mathbf{w}\)Weight vector. It is perpendicular (normal) to the hyperplane.
\(b\)Bias. It shifts the hyperplane away from the origin.
\(\mathbf{x}_i,\ y_i\)The i-th training point and its label, +1 or −1.
\(\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\)Length of w, \(\sqrt{w_1^2+w_2^2+\dots}\)
\(\xi_i\)Slack: how far point i is on the wrong side of its margin line (0 if it is fine).
\(C\)Penalty for margin violations. Large C means few violations are tolerated.
\(N\)Number of training points.

6Worked example

Given two training points: \(\mathbf{x}_1=(1,1)\) with \(y_1=-1\) and \(\mathbf{x}_2=(3,3)\) with \(y_2=+1\). Find the maximum-margin hyperplane and classify the point (4, 1).

  1. With one point per class, both are support vectors, so both lie on the margin lines: \(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_1+b=-1\) and \(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_2+b=+1\).
  2. w points along the line joining the two points, so \(\mathbf{w}=(a,a)\). Then \(2a+b=-1\) and \(6a+b=+1\).
  3. Subtract: \(4a=2\), so \(a=0.5\) and \(b=-2\).
  4. Hyperplane: \(0.5x_1+0.5x_2-2=0\), that is \(x_1+x_2=4\).
  5. Margin \(=2/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\) \(=2/\sqrt{0.5^2+0.5^2}\) \(=2/0.7071=2.828\). Check: the distance between (1,1) and (3,3) is \(\sqrt8=2.828\).
  6. Classify (4,1): \(0.5(4)+0.5(1)-2\) \(=+0.5>0\).

Answer: w = (0.5, 0.5), b = −2, margin = 2.828, and (4, 1) belongs to class +1.

7Advantages, disadvantages, applications

Advantages

  • Maximum margin gives good generalisation.
  • Works well in high-dimensional spaces.
  • Solution depends only on support vectors (sparse).
  • Convex problem: one global optimum.
  • Kernel trick handles non-linear data.

Disadvantages

  • Training is slow on large datasets (cost grows at least quadratically with the number of samples).
  • Sensitive to the choice of C and kernel.
  • Gives no direct probability output.
  • Binary by nature; multi-class needs one-vs-rest or one-vs-one.

Applications

  • Text and document classification.
  • Face detection.
  • Handwritten character recognition.
  • Bioinformatics (protein and gene classification).

9Exam corner

2 marksWhat are support vectors?

Support vectors are the training points closest to the separating hyperplane, lying on the margin boundaries \(y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i+b)=1\). They alone determine w and b; removing any other point does not change the hyperplane.

2 marksDefine margin. Why is it maximised?

Margin is the perpendicular distance between the two margin boundaries, equal to \(2/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\). A larger margin leaves more room for unseen points, so the classifier generalises better.

2 marksWhat is the role of the parameter C in SVM?

C is the penalty for margin violations. A large C allows few violations and gives a narrow margin (risk of overfitting). A small C gives a wider margin and tolerates more violations (risk of underfitting).

Topic 2 of 7 · Linear models

Logistic Regression

1One-line definition

Logistic regression is a linear classification model that passes the weighted sum \(z=\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b\) through the sigmoid function to give the probability \(P(y=1\mid\mathbf{x})\), and predicts class 1 when this probability is at least 0.5.

2Diagram to draw

3Interactive demo: weight and bias sliders

4Step-by-step explanation

In simple words

  1. Compute a score \(z=\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b\), as in linear regression.
  2. Squash the score with the sigmoid so it lies between 0 and 1.
  3. Read the result as the probability of class 1.
  4. If it is 0.5 or more, answer class 1; otherwise class 0.
  5. Training adjusts the weights until the probabilities agree with the labels of the training examples.

Technical version

  1. Model: \(P(y=1\mid\mathbf{x})=\sigma(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b)\). Equivalently, the log-odds are linear in x.
  2. \(\sigma(z)=0.5\) exactly when \(z=0\), so the decision boundary is the hyperplane \(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b=0\). It is a linear classifier.
  3. Parameters are found by maximum likelihood, which is the same as minimising the binary cross-entropy loss. This loss is convex in w.
  4. There is no closed-form solution, so gradient descent is used. The gradient is \((\hat y-y)\,\mathbf{x}\).
  5. L2 or L1 regularisation is added to avoid overfitting.
  6. For more than two classes, sigmoid is replaced by softmax (softmax regression).

5Key formulas

\[z=\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b\qquad\qquad \hat y=\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\qquad\qquad \sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)\bigl(1-\sigma(z)\bigr)\] \[\textbf{Log-odds:}\quad \ln\frac{\hat y}{1-\hat y}=\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b\] \[\textbf{Loss (binary cross-entropy):}\quad L=-\bigl[\,y\ln\hat y+(1-y)\ln(1-\hat y)\,\bigr]\] \[\textbf{Gradient and update:}\quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{w}}=(\hat y-y)\,\mathbf{x},\quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b}=\hat y-y,\qquad \mathbf{w}\leftarrow\mathbf{w}-\eta\,(\hat y-y)\,\mathbf{x}\]
SymbolMeaning
\(z\)Linear score (also called the logit).
\(\sigma\)Sigmoid (logistic) function. Output is always between 0 and 1.
\(\hat y\)Predicted probability that the class is 1.
\(y\)True label, 0 or 1.
\(\mathbf{w},\ b\)Weights and bias learned in training.
\(\eta\)Learning rate (step size).

6Worked example

Given \(\mathbf{w}=(2,-1)\), \(b=-1\), input \(\mathbf{x}=(2,1)\), true label \(y=1\), learning rate \(\eta=0.1\). Find the prediction, the loss, and the weights after one update.

  1. Score: \(z=2(2)+(-1)(1)-1=2\).
  2. Probability: \(\hat y=\sigma(2)\) \(=1/(1+e^{-2})=0.8808\). Since 0.8808 ≥ 0.5, predict class 1.
  3. Loss: \(L=-\ln(0.8808)=0.1269\).
  4. Error term: \(\hat y-y=0.8808-1=-0.1192\).
  5. Gradient: \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf{w}=(-0.1192)(2,1)\) \(=(-0.2384,\,-0.1192)\) and \(\partial L/\partial b=-0.1192\).
  6. Update: \(\mathbf{w}=(2,-1)\) \(-\,0.1(-0.2384,-0.1192)\) \(=(2.0238,\,-0.9881)\) and \(b=-1+0.0119=-0.9881\).

Answer: ŷ = 0.8808 (class 1), loss = 0.1269, new w = (2.0238, −0.9881), new b = −0.9881.

7Advantages, disadvantages, applications

Advantages

  • Simple and fast to train.
  • Outputs a probability, not just a label.
  • Weights are easy to interpret (change in log-odds).
  • Convex loss: gradient descent reaches the global minimum.

Disadvantages

  • Only a linear decision boundary.
  • Needs hand-made features for non-linear data.
  • Affected by outliers and strongly correlated features.
  • Weak on raw images, audio and text.

Applications

  • Spam or not spam.
  • Disease present or absent.
  • Credit default prediction.
  • Output layer of a neural network for binary classification.

9Exam corner

2 marksWhy is it called logistic "regression" when it is used for classification?

Because it fits a linear regression to the log-odds: \(\ln\frac{p}{1-p}=\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b\). The resulting probability p is then thresholded at 0.5 to give a class.

2 marksWrite the sigmoid function and state two of its properties.

\(\sigma(z)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\). Its output lies between 0 and 1 with \(\sigma(0)=0.5\); it is differentiable with \(\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))\), whose maximum value is 0.25.

2 marksWhy is mean squared error not used as the loss in logistic regression?

With a sigmoid output, MSE is non-convex in the weights and its gradient contains \(\sigma'(z)\), which becomes nearly zero when the neuron saturates. Cross-entropy is convex and has the simple gradient \((\hat y-y)\mathbf{x}\).

Topic 3 of 7 · Intro to neural nets

Shallow Neural Network

1One-line definition

A shallow neural network is a feed-forward network with exactly one hidden layer between the input and output layers; each neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs plus a bias and applies a non-linear activation function.

2Diagram to draw

3Interactive demo: animated forward pass

4Step-by-step explanation

In simple words

  1. A neuron multiplies each input by a weight, adds them up, and adds a bias. Call the result z.
  2. It passes z through an activation function to get its output.
  3. The hidden layer is several neurons looking at the same inputs. Each one learns a different feature.
  4. The output neuron combines the hidden outputs into the final answer.
  5. One hidden layer makes the network shallow. Two or more make it deep.

Technical version

  1. Layers: input (\(n_x\) units, no computation), hidden (\(n_h\) units), output (\(n_y\) units).
  2. Forward propagation: \(\mathbf{z}^{[1]}=W^{[1]}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}^{[1]}\), \(\mathbf{a}^{[1]}=g(\mathbf{z}^{[1]})\), then \(\mathbf{z}^{[2]}=W^{[2]}\mathbf{a}^{[1]}+\mathbf{b}^{[2]}\), \(\hat y=\sigma(\mathbf{z}^{[2]})\).
  3. The activation g must be non-linear. With a linear g the two layers collapse into \(W'\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}'\), a single linear model.
  4. Each hidden unit defines one hyperplane. The output unit combines these half-spaces into a non-linear boundary. XOR cannot be solved by a single-layer perceptron but can be solved with 2 hidden neurons.
  5. Number of parameters: \((n_x n_h+n_h)+(n_h n_y+n_y)\).
  6. Training needs a loss function, backpropagation and SGD (topics 4 to 6).

5Key formulas

\[\mathbf{z}^{[1]}=W^{[1]}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}^{[1]}\qquad \mathbf{a}^{[1]}=g\bigl(\mathbf{z}^{[1]}\bigr)\qquad \mathbf{z}^{[2]}=W^{[2]}\mathbf{a}^{[1]}+\mathbf{b}^{[2]}\qquad \hat y=\sigma\bigl(\mathbf{z}^{[2]}\bigr)\] \[\textbf{Activations:}\quad \sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\qquad \tanh(z)=\frac{e^{z}-e^{-z}}{e^{z}+e^{-z}}\qquad \operatorname{ReLU}(z)=\max(0,z)\] \[\textbf{Parameters:}\quad (n_x\,n_h+n_h)+(n_h\,n_y+n_y)\]
SymbolMeaning
\(\mathbf{x}\)Input vector.
\(W^{[1]},\mathbf{b}^{[1]}\)Weights and biases from input to hidden layer.
\(W^{[2]},\mathbf{b}^{[2]}\)Weights and biases from hidden to output layer.
\(\mathbf{z}^{[l]}\)Weighted sums (pre-activations) of layer l.
\(\mathbf{a}^{[l]}\)Outputs (activations) of layer l.
\(g\)Hidden activation function (sigmoid, tanh or ReLU).
\(n_x,n_h,n_y\)Number of input, hidden and output units.

6Worked example

A 2-2-1 network with sigmoid activations (the same network as the demo). Input \(\mathbf{x}=(1,\,0.5)\). Hidden neuron h₁: weights (0.5, −0.6), bias 0.1. Hidden neuron h₂: weights (0.3, 0.8), bias −0.2. Output neuron: weights (0.7, −0.4), bias 0.2. Find ŷ.

  1. \(z_1=0.5(1)+(-0.6)(0.5)+0.1\) \(=0.3\), so \(h_1=\sigma(0.3)=0.5744\).
  2. \(z_2=0.3(1)+0.8(0.5)-0.2\) \(=0.5\), so \(h_2=\sigma(0.5)=0.6225\).
  3. \(z_o=0.7(0.5744)\) \(+\,(-0.4)(0.6225)+0.2\) \(=0.4021-0.2490+0.2\) \(=0.3531\).
  4. \(\hat y=\sigma(0.3531)=0.5874\).
  5. Parameter count: \((2\times2+2)+(2\times1+1)=9\).

Answer: ŷ = 0.5874, so the network predicts class 1 with 58.7% confidence. It has 9 trainable parameters.

7Advantages, disadvantages, applications

Advantages

  • Learns non-linear boundaries (solves XOR).
  • Universal approximator in theory (topic 7).
  • Few parameters: fast to train, needs less data.
  • Easy to analyse and debug.

Disadvantages

  • May need a very large number of hidden neurons for complex functions.
  • No hierarchy of features (edges, parts, objects).
  • Performs poorly on raw images, speech and text compared with deep networks.

Applications

  • Small tabular classification and regression problems.
  • Function approximation and curve fitting.
  • Simple pattern recognition.

9Exam corner

2 marksWhat is a shallow neural network?

A feed-forward neural network that has only one hidden layer between its input and output layers. Each hidden neuron computes \(a=g(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b)\) with a non-linear activation g.

2 marksWhy is a non-linear activation function needed?

Without it, every layer is a linear map and the whole network reduces to a single linear model \(W'\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}'\), however many layers it has. Non-linearity lets the network learn curved decision boundaries such as XOR.

2 marksHow many trainable parameters does a 3-4-2 network have?

Input to hidden: \(3\times4+4=16\). Hidden to output: \(4\times2+2=10\). Total = 26 parameters.

Topic 4 of 7 · Connect and train a network

Loss Functions

1One-line definition

A loss function measures how far the network's prediction ŷ is from the true target y for one training example; training means adjusting the weights to minimise the average loss (the cost) over the training set.

2Diagram to draw

3Interactive demo: MSE vs cross-entropy

4Step-by-step explanation

In simple words

  1. The network makes a guess ŷ.
  2. The loss function turns the mistake into one penalty number: 0 for a perfect guess, larger for a worse guess.
  3. Training turns the weights so that the average penalty becomes small.
  4. Different tasks use different penalties. Predicting a number uses squared error. Choosing a class uses cross-entropy.

Technical version

  1. Loss \(L(y,\hat y)\) is for one example. Cost \(J(\theta)=\frac1N\sum_i L_i\) is the average over the dataset. The objective may add a regulariser to the cost.
  2. MSE is the maximum-likelihood loss when targets have Gaussian noise. It is used with a linear output unit for regression.
  3. Cross-entropy is the negative log-likelihood for a Bernoulli output (sigmoid) or a categorical output (softmax).
  4. With a sigmoid or softmax output, the cross-entropy gradient with respect to z is simply \(\hat y-y\). MSE carries an extra factor \(\sigma'(z)\), which is close to zero when the neuron saturates.
  5. Hinge loss is margin-based and is used by SVMs with labels ±1.
  6. A loss must be differentiable (almost everywhere) so that backpropagation can compute gradients.

5Key formulas

\[\textbf{Mean squared error:}\quad \text{MSE}=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_i-\hat y_i)^2\] \[\textbf{Binary cross-entropy:}\quad \text{BCE}=-\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl[\,y_i\ln\hat y_i+(1-y_i)\ln(1-\hat y_i)\,\bigr]\] \[\textbf{Categorical cross-entropy:}\quad \text{CCE}=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}y_k\ln\hat y_k\qquad\text{with softmax}\quad \hat y_k=\frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}e^{z_j}}\] \[\textbf{Hinge loss:}\quad L=\max(0,\ 1-y\,\hat y)\qquad\qquad \textbf{Mean absolute error:}\quad \text{MAE}=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}|y_i-\hat y_i|\]
SymbolMeaning
\(y_i\)True target of example i (a number for regression; 0 or 1 for binary classes; ±1 for hinge loss).
\(\hat y_i\)Prediction for example i (a probability for cross-entropy; a raw score for hinge loss).
\(N\)Number of examples.
\(K\)Number of classes.
\(y_k\)1 for the correct class and 0 for the others (one-hot vector).
\(z_k\)Raw output score of class k before softmax.

Some textbooks write the squared error for one example as \(\tfrac12(y-\hat y)^2\). The ½ cancels the 2 on differentiation; the idea is the same.

6Worked example

Three examples with true labels \(y=(1,0,1)\) and predicted probabilities \(\hat y=(0.9,\,0.2,\,0.6)\). Compute MSE and BCE.

  1. Squared errors: \((1-0.9)^2=0.01\), \((0-0.2)^2=0.04\), \((1-0.6)^2=0.16\).
  2. \(\text{MSE}=(0.01+0.04+0.16)/3\) \(=0.21/3=0.07\).
  3. Cross-entropy terms: \(-\ln0.9=0.1054\), \(-\ln(1-0.2)\) \(=-\ln0.8=0.2231\), \(-\ln0.6=0.5108\).
  4. \(\text{BCE}=\) \((0.1054+0.2231+0.5108)/3\) \(=0.8393/3=0.2798\).
  5. Multi-class case: true class is the second of three, \(\mathbf{y}=(0,1,0)\), and \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}=(0.2,0.7,0.1)\). Then \(\text{CCE}=-\ln0.7=0.3567\). Only the probability of the correct class matters.

Answer: MSE = 0.07, BCE = 0.2798, CCE = 0.3567.

7Advantages, disadvantages, applications

Advantages

  • MSE: smooth, easy to differentiate, penalises large errors strongly.
  • Cross-entropy: large gradient when the prediction is wrong; has a clear probability meaning.
  • Hinge: ignores points that are already safely correct.

Disadvantages

  • MSE: sensitive to outliers; learns slowly with sigmoid or softmax outputs.
  • Cross-entropy: undefined at ln 0, so predictions must be clipped; punishes mislabelled data heavily.
  • Hinge: not differentiable at the kink; gives no probabilities.

Applications

  • MSE, MAE: regression (price, temperature, age).
  • BCE: two-class problems (spam, disease).

A loss function measures the error between the prediction ŷ and the target y for one training example. The cost function is the average of the loss over all training examples, and it is the quantity minimised during training.

2 marksWhy is cross-entropy preferred to MSE for classification?

With a sigmoid or softmax output, the cross-entropy gradient is \(\hat y-y\), which stays large when the prediction is badly wrong. The MSE gradient contains \(\sigma'(z)\), which is almost zero for saturated neurons, so learning becomes slow.

\(L=\max(0,\,1-y\,\hat y)\) with \(y\in\{-1,+1\}\) and \(\hat y\) the raw score. It is the loss of the soft-margin SVM: it is zero for points correctly classified with margin at least 1.

Backpropagation

1One-line definition

Backpropagation is the algorithm that computes the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight of a network by applying the chain rule backwards, from the output layer to the input layer, reusing each layer's error term δ for the layer before it.

2Diagram to draw

3Interactive demo: chain rule, one step at a time

4Step-by-step explanation

In simple words

  1. Forward pass: feed the input and get the prediction ŷ.
  2. Compare ŷ with the target to get the loss.
  3. Ask how much each weight contributed to the error. Start with the weights nearest the output.
  4. Pass the error backwards. Each hidden neuron receives a share of the blame in proportion to the weight that connects it to the output.
  5. Nudge every weight a little in the direction that reduces the error. Repeat with the next example.

Technical version

  1. Forward pass: compute and store \(\mathbf{z}^{[l]}\) and \(\mathbf{a}^{[l]}\) for every layer.
  2. Output error: \(\delta^{[L]}=\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{a}^{[L]}}\odot g'(\mathbf{z}^{[L]})\).
  3. Backward recursion: \(\delta^{[l]}=\bigl(W^{[l+1]}\bigr)^{\!\top}\delta^{[l+1]}\odot g'(\mathbf{z}^{[l]})\).
  4. Gradients: \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial W^{[l]}}=\delta^{[l]}\bigl(\mathbf{a}^{[l-1]}\bigr)^{\!\top}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{b}^{[l]}}=\delta^{[l]}\).
  5. Update each parameter with gradient descent: \(W\leftarrow W-\eta\,\partial L/\partial W\).
  6. Efficiency: every δ is reused, so all gradients cost about one forward plus one backward pass. Perturbing each weight separately would need one forward pass per weight.

Backpropagation is not the same as gradient descent. Backpropagation only computes the gradients; gradient descent (or SGD) uses them to update the weights. The method was made popular by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams in 1986.

5Key formulas

\[\textbf{Chain rule for one weight:}\quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial w}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\hat y}\cdot\frac{\partial\hat y}{\partial z}\cdot\frac{\partial z}{\partial w}\] \[\textbf{Output layer:}\quad \delta_o=(\hat y-y)\,\hat y(1-\hat y)\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial v_j}=\delta_o\,h_j\] \[\textbf{Hidden layer:}\quad \delta_j=\delta_o\,v_j\,h_j(1-h_j)\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{ji}}=\delta_j\,x_i\] \[\textbf{Update:}\quad w\leftarrow w-\eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}\]

The output and hidden formulas above are for sigmoid activations and the loss \(L=\tfrac12(y-\hat y)^2\), which is the case usually asked in exams.

\(\delta_j\)Error term of hidden neuron j, \(\partial L/\partial z_j\).
\(v_j\)Weight from hidden neuron j to the output neuron.
\(w_{ji}\)Weight from input i to hidden neuron j.
\(h_j\)Output of hidden neuron j.
\(\hat y(1-\hat y)\)Derivative of the sigmoid at the output neuron.

7Advantages, disadvantages, applications

Advantages

  • Efficient: all gradients in one forward and one backward pass.
  • General: works for any differentiable network.
  • Simple to implement with matrix operations.
  • The basis of training for almost all deep learning models.

Disadvantages

  • Vanishing or exploding gradients in deep networks.
  • Needs differentiable activations and labelled data.
  • Sensitive to weight initialisation and learning rate.
  • The loss surface is non-convex, so the global minimum is not guaranteed.

  • Training multi-layer perceptrons.
  • Training CNNs for images.
  • Training RNNs and LSTMs (backpropagation through time).

2 marksWhat is backpropagation?

Backpropagation is an algorithm that computes the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight in a neural network by applying the chain rule from the output layer back to the input layer. The gradients are then used by gradient descent to update the weights.

2 marksState the chain rule as used in backpropagation.

\(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w}=\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\hat y}\cdot\dfrac{\partial\hat y}{\partial z}\cdot\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial w}\). The effect of a weight on the loss is the product of the local derivatives along the path from that weight to the loss.

2 marksWhat is the vanishing gradient problem?

During backpropagation the gradient is multiplied by \(g'(z)\) at every layer. For the sigmoid this is at most 0.25, so in a deep network the gradient shrinks exponentially and the early layers learn very slowly. ReLU, good initialisation and batch normalisation reduce the problem.

Gradient Descent and Stochastic Gradient Descent

1One-line definition

Gradient descent minimises the loss by repeatedly moving the weights a small step, set by the learning rate η, in the direction opposite to the gradient. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) estimates that gradient from one randomly chosen training example (or a small mini-batch) per update instead of the whole dataset.

2Diagram to draw

3Interactive demo: three paths on a loss contour plot

4Step-by-step explanation

In simple words

  1. Think of the loss as a hilly landscape. The weights are your position; the height is the error.
  2. The gradient points uphill. Step the other way to go down.
  3. The learning rate is the length of your step. Too small is slow. Too large overshoots the valley.
  4. Batch GD looks at the whole dataset before each step. SGD looks at one random example and steps at once.
  5. Mini-batch GD looks at a small group (say 32 examples). It is the method used in practice.

Technical version

  1. Update rule: \(\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\).
  2. Batch GD uses the exact gradient over all N examples: one update per epoch, expensive for large N.
  3. SGD uses the gradient of one random example. It is an unbiased but noisy estimate of the true gradient: N updates per epoch.
  4. Mini-batch GD averages over m examples (commonly 32 to 256). The noise falls as m grows, and the batch can be processed in parallel on a GPU.
  5. The noise in SGD helps it leave saddle points and shallow local minima, but the loss fluctuates. A decaying learning rate lets it settle.
  6. Data is shuffled every epoch so that batches are random.

5Key formulas

\[\textbf{Update rule:}\quad \theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\] \[\textbf{Batch GD:}\quad \nabla_\theta J=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla_\theta L_i\] \[\textbf{SGD:}\quad \nabla_\theta J\approx\nabla_\theta L_i\quad(\text{one random example } i)\] \[\textbf{Mini-batch GD:}\quad \nabla_\theta J\approx\frac1m\sum_{i\in B}\nabla_\theta L_i\] \[\textbf{Updates per epoch}=\frac{N}{\text{batch size}}\]
SymbolMeaning
\(\theta\)All trainable parameters (weights and biases).
\(\eta\)Learning rate: the step size.
\(J(\theta)\)Cost: average loss over the training set.
\(\nabla_\theta J\)Gradient: vector of partial derivatives of J with respect to each parameter.
\(L_i\)Loss on training example i.
\(N\)Number of training examples.
\(B,\ m\)A randomly chosen mini-batch and its size.

6Worked example

Part A. Minimise \(J(w)=(w-3)^2\) by gradient descent starting from \(w_0=0\) with \(\eta=0.1\). Part B. A dataset has 1000 examples. How many updates per epoch for batch GD, mini-batch GD with batch size 50, and SGD?

  1. Gradient: \(dJ/dw=2(w-3)\).
  2. Step 1: gradient at \(w_0=0\) is \(-6\). \(w_1=0-0.1(-6)=0.6\).
  3. Step 2: gradient at 0.6 is \(-4.8\). \(w_2=0.6+0.48=1.08\).
  4. Step 3: gradient at 1.08 is \(-3.84\). \(w_3=1.08+0.384=1.464\).
  5. Each step closes 20% of the remaining distance to 3, so w converges to the minimum at \(w=3\).
  6. Part B: batch GD makes \(1000/1000=1\) update per epoch; mini-batch makes \(1000/50=20\); SGD makes \(1000/1=1000\).

Answer: w goes 0 → 0.6 → 1.08 → 1.464 → … → 3. Updates per epoch: 1 (batch), 20 (mini-batch of 50), 1000 (SGD).

7Advantages, disadvantages, applications (of SGD)

Advantages

  • Each update is cheap and fast.
  • Needs little memory; works with very large or streaming data.
  • Starts improving after seeing only a few examples.
  • Noise helps escape saddle points and shallow local minima.

Disadvantages

  • Noisy path; the loss fluctuates.
  • Does not settle exactly at the minimum with a fixed learning rate.
  • One example at a time cannot use GPU parallelism.
  • Sensitive to the learning rate.

Applications

  • Training almost all deep networks (as mini-batch SGD).
  • Online learning where data arrives continuously.
  • Base of the optimisers Momentum, RMSProp and Adam (Unit IV).

2 marksWhat is the learning rate? What happens if it is too large or too small?

The learning rate η is the step size used in the update \(w\leftarrow w-\eta\,\partial L/\partial w\). If it is too large, the updates overshoot the minimum and may diverge. If it is too small, training is very slow and may get stuck.

2 marksDifferentiate epoch, batch size and iteration.

An epoch is one full pass over the training set. Batch size is the number of examples used for one weight update. An iteration is one weight update. Iterations per epoch = N / batch size.

2 marksWhy is SGD called "stochastic"?

Because each update uses a randomly chosen example (or mini-batch), so the gradient is a random, noisy estimate of the true gradient. It is correct on average but varies from step to step.

Neural Networks as Universal Function Approximators

1One-line definition

The universal approximation theorem states that a feed-forward network with a single hidden layer containing a finite number of neurons and a non-linear (non-polynomial) activation can approximate any continuous function on a closed and bounded domain to any desired accuracy.

2Diagram to draw

3Interactive demo: add neurons one by one

4Step-by-step explanation

In simple words

  1. One sigmoid neuron with a large weight looks like a step: 0 on the left, 1 on the right.
  2. Subtract two steps placed side by side and you get a bump: a short tower.
  3. Give each tower its own height and stand many towers next to each other. Together they trace any smooth curve, like a bar chart.
  4. Narrower towers need more neurons and give a smaller error.
  5. So one hidden layer with enough neurons can copy any continuous function as closely as you like.

Technical version

  1. The network has the form \(F(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_i\,\sigma(\mathbf{w}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}+b_i)\): one hidden layer and a linear output.
  2. Theorem: for any continuous f on a compact set K and any \(\varepsilon\gt 0\), there is such an F with \(|F(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})|\lt\varepsilon\) for all x in K.
  3. Cybenko (1989) proved it for sigmoidal activations. Hornik (1991) showed that the multilayer architecture, not the particular activation, gives the property. Leshno and co-authors (1993) showed it holds exactly when the activation is not a polynomial, which includes ReLU.
  4. It is an existence result. It does not say how many neurons are needed, how to find the weights, or how well the network will generalise.
  5. The number of neurons needed can be extremely large. Deep networks can represent many functions with far fewer neurons, which is the motivation for depth (Unit II).

5Key formulas

\[\textbf{Network:}\quad F(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_i\,\sigma(\mathbf{w}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}+b_i)\] \[\textbf{Theorem:}\quad \forall\,\varepsilon\gt 0\ \ \exists\,N,\ v_i,\ \mathbf{w}_i,\ b_i\ \ \text{such that}\quad |F(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})|\lt\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{x}\in K\] \[\textbf{Bump from two neurons:}\quad \sigma\bigl(s(x-a)\bigr)-\sigma\bigl(s(x-b)\bigr)\approx\begin{cases}1 & a\lt x\lt b\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad(s\text{ large})\]
SymbolMeaning
\(f\)The target: any continuous function.
\(F\)The function computed by the network.
\(N\)Number of hidden neurons.
\(\mathbf{w}_i,\ b_i\)Input weights and bias of hidden neuron i.
\(v_i\)Output weight of hidden neuron i (the height of its step).
\(\varepsilon\)The allowed error, as small as you wish.
\(K\)A compact (closed and bounded) region of inputs, such as [0, 1].
\(s\)Steepness of the sigmoid. Larger s gives a sharper step.

6Worked example

Build a bump on the interval (0.4, 0.6) from two sigmoid neurons with steepness \(s=100\), then use bumps to approximate \(f(x)=x^2\) on [0, 1].

  1. Bump: \(B(x)=\sigma\bigl(100(x-0.4)\bigr)\) \(-\,\sigma\bigl(100(x-0.6)\bigr)\).
  2. Inside, at \(x=0.5\): \(\sigma(10)-\sigma(-10)\) \(=0.99995-0.00005\) \(=0.9999\approx1\).
  3. Outside, at \(x=0.2\): \(\sigma(-20)-\sigma(-40)\approx0\).
  4. Split [0, 1] into 5 intervals of width 0.2 with centres 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9. Use one bump per interval: \(5\times2=10\) hidden neurons.
  5. Set each bump's height to f at the centre: \(0.01,\ 0.09,\ 0.25,\ 0.49,\ 0.81\).
  6. Check at \(x=0.52\): the network gives 0.25 and the true value is \(0.52^2=0.2704\). Error 0.02.
  7. Using 50 intervals (100 neurons) makes each step ten times narrower, and the error falls about ten times.

Answer: two neurons make one bump; 10 neurons give a 5-step approximation of x²; more neurons give a smaller error.

7Significance, limitations, applications

Significance

  • Gives the theoretical reason neural networks are so flexible.
  • Needs only one hidden layer and a non-linear activation.
  • Works for any continuous function, with no assumption on its form.

Limitations

  • Existence only: it gives no training method.
  • Does not say how many neurons; the number may be impractically large.
  • Says nothing about generalisation to unseen data.
  • Covers continuous functions on bounded domains only.

Applications

  • Function approximation and regression.
  • Modelling unknown non-linear systems (control, time series).
  • Justifies using networks as general-purpose models in vision, speech and language.

1 · SVM

  • Linear classifier with the maximum margin.
  • Hyperplane \(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b=0\); margin \(2/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\).
  • Minimise \(\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2\) s.t. \(y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i+b)\ge1\).
  • Support vectors: points on the margin; they alone fix the line.
  • Soft margin: slack \(\xi_i\), penalty C, hinge loss.

2 · Logistic regression

  • \(\hat y=\sigma(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b)\), \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\).
  • Output is \(P(y=1\mid\mathbf{x})\); class 1 if ≥ 0.5.
  • Loss: binary cross-entropy; gradient \((\hat y-y)\mathbf{x}\).
  • One sigmoid neuron. Linear boundary.

3 · Shallow network

  • Exactly one hidden layer.
  • \(\mathbf{a}^{[1]}=g(W^{[1]}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}^{[1]})\), \(\hat y=\sigma(W^{[2]}\mathbf{a}^{[1]}+\mathbf{b}^{[2]})\).
  • Non-linear g is essential; otherwise the net is linear.
  • Parameters: \((n_xn_h+n_h)+(n_hn_y+n_y)\).
  • Sigmoid (0,1), tanh (−1,1), ReLU max(0,z).

4 · Loss functions

  • Loss: one example. Cost: average. Objective: cost + regulariser.
  • MSE \(=\frac1N\sum(y-\hat y)^2\) for regression.
  • BCE \(=-[y\ln\hat y+(1-y)\ln(1-\hat y)]\) for 2 classes.
  • CCE \(=-\sum y_k\ln\hat y_k\) with softmax.
  • Cross-entropy gradient \(\hat y-y\): no slowdown.

5 · Backpropagation

  • Chain rule applied from output back to input.
  • \(\delta_o=(\hat y-y)\hat y(1-\hat y)\); \(\partial L/\partial v_j=\delta_oh_j\).
  • \(\delta_j=\delta_ov_jh_j(1-h_j)\); \(\partial L/\partial w_{ji}=\delta_jx_i\).
  • Steps: Forward, Loss, Backward, Update.
  • Problem: vanishing gradient (sigmoid slope ≤ 0.25).

6 · Gradient descent, SGD

  • \(\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\nabla_\theta J\).
  • Batch: all N, smooth, 1 update per epoch.
  • SGD: 1 example, noisy, N updates per epoch.
  • Mini-batch: m examples, N/m updates; used in practice.
  • η too large diverges; too small is slow.

7 · Universal approximation

  • One hidden layer + non-linear activation + enough neurons can approximate any continuous function on a compact set.
  • \(F(\mathbf{x})=\sum v_i\sigma(\mathbf{w}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}+b_i)\), \(|F-f|\lt\varepsilon\).
  • Cybenko 1989; Hornik 1991.
  • Limits: Existence only, Number unknown, Training not guaranteed.
